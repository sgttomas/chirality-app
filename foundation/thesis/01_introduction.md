# Chapter 1 — Introduction

---

## 1.1 The Problem

Large language models can draft engineering documents, extract dependencies from scope-of-work descriptions, reconcile conflicting specifications, generate cost estimates, and produce analysis reports that read as though a competent engineer wrote them. This capability is transformative. It is also, in its raw form, incompatible with regulated professional engineering practice.

The incompatibility is not only that LLMs produce bad output—though they
sometimes do. LLM output carries no intrinsic epistemic warrant. Correct and
incorrect claims are generated by the same mechanism and may be
indistinguishable by surface inspection alone: fluent prose, specific values,
and confident tone. The model's output is not self-certifying and cannot be
assumed grounded merely because it reads that way.

Professional engineering practice is a duty-of-care regime. A licensed professional who authenticates a professional work product — who stamps, seals, and issues it for reliance — is accepting personal, non-delegable responsibility for its accuracy, completeness, and fitness for purpose. This authentication act, under the *Engineering and Geoscience Professions Act* (Alberta) and APEGA practice standards, requires that the professional has either directly supervised and controlled the work or conducted a thorough review of it. Both mechanisms presuppose that the professional can assess the epistemic grounding of the claims in the work — that they can distinguish what is known from what is assumed, what is extracted from what is inferred, and what is supported by evidence from what is not.

An LLM output, absent architectural controls, does not support this assessment. The professional faces a body of text that reads as authoritative but provides no provenance, no epistemic labels, and no indication of where evidence ends and interpolation begins. The thorough review required by regulation becomes, in effect, a complete independent re-derivation — which eliminates the productivity benefit of using AI in the first place.

This thesis addresses the question: **how can systems engineering principles be applied to govern LLM-based agent systems such that their outputs can be relied upon in regulated professional engineering practice?**

---

## 1.2 The Approach

The response presented in this thesis is not to rely on model improvement
alone, though such improvements are welcome and complementary. It is to
engineer a governance architecture that requires represented claim states and
grounds to be inspectable so that a qualified professional can determine what
to rely on.

The architecture, called Chirality, is a formally specified agent operating system for deliverable-heavy professional work. It operates on a foundational decision: all project state lives in git-tracked plain files. There is no external database. The filesystem is the single source of truth. This decision is load-bearing — every capability the system provides (traceability, immutable snapshots, content-addressed approval, the complete audit trail) depends on it.

Agents occupy three runtime delegation positions: Agent 0 (Supervising Architect) aligns with the human and supervises managers; Agent 1 (Manager) orchestrates gate-controlled workflows; Agent 2 (Specialist) executes sealed bounded briefs and returns auditable outputs without delegating. Normative standards constrain all three positions from outside the hierarchy. Authority and capability do not increase through delegation, escalation flows upward, and no agent may bypass a human gate.

The architecture is governed by three layers of formally stated invariants — workflow design requirements (R1–R17), decomposition invariants (I1–I10), and system-wide invariants (K-*) — enforced through agent instructions, runtime orchestration, human gates, and deterministic tooling.

The system has been implemented as a desktop application with an indexed agent suite, a registered deterministic toolset, and a formal governance document suite. Active membership is maintained in the source registries rather than mutable count prose. It has been grounded in the APEGA regulatory framework through a professional practice standard that maps the architecture to the specific requirements of the *Relying on the Work of Others and Outsourcing* practice standard.

Taken as a whole, Chirality is best categorized as a governed application environment for agent-assisted professional work. The agent operating system is one contained component; around it, the same enclosure holds the deterministic toolset, the governed project records, domain applications where a discipline requires deterministic computation (the domain-engine boundary, governed by invariants K-DOMAIN-1 through K-DOMAIN-4; Chapter 4, §4.7 and §4.10), and the human gates through which authority is exercised. The distinction matters because the thesis's claims attach to the environment, not to any single component: agents propose, deterministic tools and domain engines compute, humans rule, and the versioned record binds what was decided. What the environment refuses to do is as significant as what it does — it provides no conforming path by which machine output becomes professional authority.

## 1.3 The Five Contributions

This thesis makes five novel contributions:

**1. An epistemic architecture for LLM reliability transparency.** Four mechanisms — mandatory provenance, no-invention rules, conflict surfacing, and epistemic labeling — make the epistemic status of every claim produced by the system transparent and auditable. The system does not try to prevent hallucination; it makes unsupported claims structurally visible. This responds to a gap identified in the literature: existing approaches focus on reducing hallucination rates (a population-level improvement), while professional practice requires per-claim epistemic transparency.

**2. A regulatory mapping grounding AI agent governance in existing professional obligations.** The thesis argues that APEGA's *Relying on the Work of Others and Outsourcing* standard can be interpreted to govern AI agent use because the professional's obligations are defined by what the professional must do, not by what the worker is. This interpretation shows that AI agent governance may be achievable within existing regulatory frameworks, without requiring new AI-specific regulation, though it remains jurisdiction- and regulator-dependent.

**3. A four-pillar philosophical framework with fractal instantiation.** The
architecture rests on ontology (what exists), epistemology (what is
warranted), praxiology (how work is done), and axiology (what values govern).
The epistemology is the load-bearing pillar and retains six operational
primitives (claim, warrant, status, gap, conflict, ruling) and the warrant
lifecycle (UNWARRANTED → CITED → REVIEWED → AUTHENTICATED). The framework
locates knowledge in a situated knower and treats authentication as an
attributable relation of accountable reliance on identified information. The
four-document production kit mirrors the four accountability questions—a
fractal property of the architecture, not a claim that one chiral duality
recurs at every level.

**4. A fault containment architecture through write scope quarantine.** The Agent 0/1/2 runtime hierarchy combined with declared write scopes creates formal fault containment zones. Under the declared write-scope contract, a Type 2 agent failure is contained away from source truth. This adapts classical SE fault containment to the governance of probabilistic agents.

**5. A three-layer invariant system with four-layer enforcement.** Three layers of formally stated invariants — the workflow design requirements (R1–R17), the decomposition invariants (I1–I10), and the system-wide K-* catalog maintained in `CONTRACT.md` — are enforced through agent instructions, runtime orchestration, human gates, and deterministic tooling. This constitutes a formal methods contribution applied to a novel domain: the governance of LLM-based agent systems.

## 1.4 Thesis Structure

The thesis is organized as follows:

- **Chapter 2 (Literature Review)** surveys five domains — AI agent architectures, LLM reliability, formal methods and safety-critical SE, professional regulation, and epistemic frameworks — and identifies the gap that motivates this work.
- **Chapter 3 (Philosophical Framework)** presents the four pillars, argues that the epistemology is load-bearing, and demonstrates the fractal property.
- **Chapter 4 (Architecture)** presents the technical architecture: filesystem-as-state, entity model, lifecycle state machine, type hierarchy, write scope, invariant system, orchestration, and contracts.
- **Chapter 5 (Epistemic Architecture)** develops the core novelty claim: the six epistemic primitives, the four enforcement mechanisms, the warrant lifecycle as operational model, worked examples, and comparison to alternative approaches.
- **Chapter 6 (Professional Practice Integration)** presents the APEGA regulatory mapping and the argument that AI agents are "others" under §3.0.
- **Chapter 7 (SE Design Analysis)** examines the architecture through eight SE disciplines.
- **Chapter 8 (Implementation and Validation)** presents the working implementation: the indexed agent suite, the registered toolset, the desktop application, and the validation mechanisms.
- **Chapter 9 (Discussion)** assesses limitations, generalizability, and future work.
- **Chapter 10 (Conclusion)** restates the contributions and answers the research question.

Appendix A provides the full invariant catalog. Appendix C provides the detailed APEGA regulatory mapping. Appendix D presents the philosophical conjecture underlying Chapter 3's framework. The current agent index is maintained in the live `AGENTS.md` registry.

## 1.5 Scope and Limitations

This thesis presents the architecture, its philosophical foundation, its regulatory grounding, and its implementation. It does not present:

- Empirical validation through controlled studies with practicing engineers (identified as the highest-priority future work in Chapter 9)
- A regulatory ruling or endorsement by APEGA (the regulatory mapping is the firm's interpretation, not a regulator's pronouncement)
- A claim that the architecture eliminates LLM hallucination (it makes hallucination visible, not absent)
- A claim that instruction-level enforcement is sufficient alone (the architecture relies on multi-layer defense in depth)

The contribution is architectural, not empirical. The thesis demonstrates that a governance architecture for AI agents in professional practice can be designed, specified, implemented, and grounded in existing regulation. Whether it improves professional review in measured practice is a question for the empirical study that this architecture makes possible.
